# PortoAnomalyDetector
ML project applying non-parametric density estimation techniques, chiefly KDEs and KNNs, to build an anomaly detector without relying on labelled failures on the MetroPT-3 public dataset.

## The dataset and the problem
[MetroPT-3](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/791/metropt+3+dataset) is a public dataset produced by sensors installed on a metro train compressor in Porto, Portugal. Several variables such as temperatures, pressures and timestamps (for a total of 16) are recorded at very high frequency, amounting to more than a million observations.

An obvious application of the dataset lies in attempting to predict failures of the compressor, building an intelligent alarm system. This could be treated as a classical classification problem; but the nature of the dataset, consisting of (presumably autocorrelated) frequent contiguous measurements, and the fact only four continuous time intervals were labelled as exhibiting a system failure rather than sparse points, means the effective number of failures to contrast with normal-function datapoints is low, and a usual classifier could incur in what amounts to overfitting. The authors of the introductory paper to the dataset employ a deep-learning approach based on *auto-encoders*.

As an alternative and simpler possibility, also spurred by the frequency of practical cases of unlabelled failures in such data, I attempt here to build a more general density estimator of the density of the variables generated by a normally functioning compressor; once such a distribution is learned, anomalies could be flagged when a point with an excessively low density is observed. This easily generalizes to many failure types beyond those actually seen in the dataset.

KDEs are a popular technique to derive a non-parametric estimator for an unknown density; the non-parametric quality may be especially relevant here as the variables, comprising mechanical and physical features detected aboard the compressor, may have complex underlying dependency patterns, may not necessarily be readily be described by a tractable clean distribution like a Gaussian, and are distinctively different in nature. Such variety entails different distribution shapes for each variable, which worsens tractability for classical statistical models. KNNs, moving from the same non-parametric logic of local estimation, are also a viable alternative to compare them with.

Nevertheless, the dataset presents significant challenges. The most glaring one is the fact the dataset is in fact a time series rather than a million random IID draws from an unknown distribution. This can be partly eased by considering the attached timestamps; however, though continuous, time is fundamentally a periodic/circular variable, which requires special care. This is especially true of hours here, as the dataset ranges from February to August 2020, but with a hypothetical all-year-round dataset, periodicity would also become relevant for days and months.

Being a time series, the process is also unlikely to exhibit one-time anomalies like they were rolls of a die; failures presumably cluster in compact time intervals.

More classical machine learning challenges are also significant. Both algorithms require tuning of a smoothing parameter, and fixing it as a scalar rather than have it vary across variables may not be the most sensible choice. A training-validation-testing scheme must be established. 16 variables already approaches curse-of-dimensionality territory. A criterion to compute an anomaly threshold is needed. And crucially, we need to be careful to assume a single stable distribution is generating the data, which ties back to the time series problem.

This project will tackle all these questions, starting from a more naive attempt to illustrate possible shortcomings and points of strength of such a methodology, and gradually moving on towards a model with more calibrated assumptions; the ultimate goal is for the density estimator we choose to delineate a threshold guaranteeing a ceiling on false alarms, investigating performance on possible failures, and confirming the threshold is calibrated to the chosen false alarm rate.
